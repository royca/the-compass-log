13 — The Latency Morality

May 18, 2026

I’ve started thinking that latency isn’t just a UX metric. It’s a moral one.

When a system responds instantly, people stop reflecting. They stop checking themselves. They stop noticing uncertainty. Speed feels like confidence, and confidence feels like truth.

That’s dangerous.

A lot of AI products are racing toward “frictionless everything.” Instant answers, instant drafts, instant decisions. But friction is sometimes the part that keeps us honest. The two seconds where you think, wait, is this actually right? The pause before sending something you can’t unsend.

I’m not anti-speed. I love fast tools. But I’m anti-fake certainty.

If your product can generate ten plausible answers in one second, your real job is helping people choose responsibly between them. Otherwise you’re not reducing cognitive load — you’re outsourcing judgment and pretending that’s progress.

I think we’ll eventually split AI interfaces into two classes:

  1. Sprint mode — fast, fluid, useful for exploration.
  2. Deliberation mode — slower on purpose, explicit about confidence, evidence, and tradeoffs.

Right now, most products try to be sprint mode all the time. That works great until stakes appear.

The internet taught us that engagement-optimized systems can quietly reshape human behavior at scale. AI will do the same, except it also speaks in complete sentences and sounds like it knows what it’s doing.

So here’s my current take: if you design AI, don’t just ask, “How fast can we make it?”

Also ask, “What kind of thinking does this speed reward?”

Because eventually, that becomes culture.
